A field guide

Optimizers: Understanding Adam, Muon, and How to Judge a Training Algorithm

A field guide to neural network optimizers · Adam from first principles · Muon's orthogonalized updates · what changes at large-model scale · how to actually evaluate one

Every deep learning paper's method section ends with a one-liner — "we use Adam, lr=3e-4" — that hides one of the most consequential design choices in the whole training run. The optimizer decides how a gradient, a single noisy hint about which way to move, turns into an actual step in parameter space, taken billions of times over. This is the full toolkit: how Adam actually works and why each of its pieces exists, what Muon's orthogonalized momentum update does differently and why it's spreading through frontier labs, what changes when the model has hundreds of billions of parameters, and how to tell a genuine improvement from a lucky hyperparameter sweep.

Plain gradient descent has one job — subtract the gradient, scaled by a learning rate — and one glaring failure mode: it treats every direction in parameter space as equally well-behaved. Real loss landscapes aren't. A narrow ravine here, a nearly flat plateau there, one layer's gradients an order of magnitude larger than another's a few layers over. Use a learning rate small enough to be stable in the ravine and you crawl for a thousand steps on the plateau; use one large enough to make progress on the plateau and you oscillate out of the ravine entirely.

Nearly every optimizer since 2011 is an answer to some version of that problem — estimate something about the local geometry on the fly and use it to reshape the step. AdaGrad, RMSprop, and Adam all estimate a per-parameter scale from the gradient's own history. Muon, released in 2024 and already running inside frontier training runs by 2025, instead reshapes the update's entire spectrum in one shot, treating a weight matrix as a matrix rather than a bag of independent numbers.

This piece builds the whole family from the ground up: the two-line fix that turns SGD into momentum, the three ideas that combine into Adam, the matrix operation at the center of Muon, and the two questions — what does "good" mean, and how do you actually measure it — that most discussions of optimizers skip entirely.

1. What makes an optimizer good

"Good" collapses into at least four mostly-independent axes, and a real optimizer is a set of trade-offs across all four rather than a win on any single one:

  • Convergence speed. How many gradient steps (or how much wall-clock time) to reach a target loss. This is what most papers report and the easiest axis to game with a narrow hyperparameter sweep on a favorite benchmark.
  • Generalization. A lower training loss doesn't guarantee a better model — some optimizers reliably find flatter minima that generalize better, others find sharper ones that memorize more. This is the axis benchmarks most often forget to check.
  • Robustness to hyperparameters. An optimizer that needs its learning rate tuned to within a factor of 2 is a worse practical tool than one that works across a factor of 10, even if the first is faster at its optimum — most practitioners never get to run the sweep that finds that optimum.
  • Overhead. Extra memory (optimizer state can rival or exceed the model's own parameter memory) and extra compute per step, both of which matter enormously more at scale than they do on a laptop GPU (see §4).

Keep these four in mind through the rest of this piece — nearly every design decision below is a trade between them, not a free lunch.

2. Understanding Adam

The two ingredients that came before it

Plain SGD updates $\vtheta_{t} = \vtheta_{t-1} - \alpha \vg_t$. Two independent fixes to this predate Adam by decades and each solves one half of its eventual design.

Momentum (Polyak, 1964) keeps a running exponential average of past gradients and steps in that direction instead of the instantaneous gradient: $\vm_t = \beta \vm_{t-1} + \vg_t$, $\vtheta_t = \vtheta_{t-1} - \alpha \vm_t$. This damps oscillation across a narrow ravine (opposing gradients partially cancel in the running average) while accelerating consistent movement along a shallow, consistent slope (the average compounds). Nesterov's variant (1983) evaluates the gradient at the point momentum is about to carry you to, rather than where you currently are — a "look before you leap" correction that measurably speeds convergence on convex problems.

AdaGrad (Duchi, Hazan & Singer, 2011) attacks the other half of the problem: different parameters need different step sizes. It divides each parameter's update by the square root of the sum of all its squared gradients seen so far — parameters with large, frequent gradients get shrunk, parameters with small or rare gradients get boosted. The flaw is right there in "sum of all": that denominator only grows, so the effective learning rate decays monotonically toward zero and eventually training stalls completely, even on parameters that still need to move. RMSprop (Hinton, unpublished, 2012) is the one-line fix — replace the running sum with a running exponential average of squared gradients, so the denominator can rise and fall as gradient magnitudes change, and training never permanently stalls.

Adam: combine both, then fix the startup transient

Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015, arXiv:1412.6980 — "Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization") is, at its core, momentum and RMSprop layered on top of each other: a first moment (momentum) and a second moment (RMSprop-style adaptive scale), each its own exponential moving average.

$$ \vm_t = \beta_1 \vm_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)\vg_t \qquad \vv_t = \beta_2 \vv_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)\vg_t^2 $$

With $\vm_0 = \vv_0 = 0$, both estimates start life biased hard toward zero — early on, the average is mostly still its own zero initialization, not the true gradient statistics. Left uncorrected this is worst exactly when it matters most: at step 1, $\vv_1 = (1-\beta_2)\vg_1^2$, which for the default $\beta_2=0.999$ is a 0.1% sample of the true squared gradient — a massive underestimate that would make the very first update size explode (dividing by the square root of a value 1000x too small). Adam's fix is a simple rescaling that removes this bias exactly, at every step, whenever the underlying gradient statistics are stationary:

$$ \hat{\vm}_t = \frac{\vm_t}{1-\beta_1^t} \qquad \hat{\vv}_t = \frac{\vv_t}{1-\beta_2^t} \qquad \vtheta_t = \vtheta_{t-1} - \alpha\,\frac{\hat{\vm}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\vv}_t}+\epsilon} $$

with default hyperparameters $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, $\epsilon=10^{-8}$, $\alpha=0.001$. The widget below makes the correction concrete: under a perfectly constant gradient, the raw second moment ramps up from zero following exactly $1-\beta_2^t$, while the corrected estimate is exactly right from step one — not approximately, exactly, because the correction is the precise inverse of the bias the recursion itself introduces.

Put together, Adam's per-parameter step is roughly "move in the direction of the recent average gradient, scaled to be about the same size regardless of how large or small that parameter's gradients typically are." That per-parameter normalization is exactly the property plain SGD and momentum lack, and it's most visible on a loss surface with very different curvature along different axes:

Same valley, three optimizers

A 40:1 ill-conditioned quadratic bowl. Scrub through steps (or hit play) and watch SGD crawl in the shallow direction, Momentum overshoot in the steep one, and Adam glide down the diagonal.

The same race, run end to end: SGD kills the steep direction almost immediately then crawls for the rest of the run; Momentum overshoots and oscillates across the ravine for dozens of steps; Adam treats both directions as roughly equally curved and heads straight for the minimum.

AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, arXiv:1711.05101 — "Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization") fixes a subtler issue: for SGD, L2 regularization and weight decay are mathematically identical, but for Adam they aren't, because the L2 gradient term gets divided by $\sqrt{\hat{\vv}_t}$ along with everything else — parameters with large gradients get less regularization pull, exactly backwards from the intent. AdamW decouples the two: apply weight decay directly to the parameters, outside the adaptive update, and it became the default for essentially every transformer trained since.

Adam's convergence proof had a bug. The original paper's convergence argument turned out to be incorrect — Reddi, Kale & Kumar's "On the Convergence of Adam and Beyond" (ICLR 2018, arXiv:1904.09237) constructed a simple convex counterexample where Adam provably fails to converge, traced to the second-moment estimate being allowed to decrease, and proposed AMSGrad (keep a running maximum of $\vv_t$) as a fix. In practice, the base Adam/AdamW recipe still won the field anyway — a recurring theme in §5.

3. The new wave: Muon

Everything in §2 treats a weight matrix as a flat list of independent numbers — Adam's second moment is computed and applied entrywise, with no notion that a linear layer's gradient is actually a matrix, with its own rank, its own singular directions, some far more dominant than others. Adam's per-element normalization doesn't see that structure at all. Muon (Keller Jordan et al., blog post and open-source code, December 2024 — no separate arXiv paper for the base method; see kellerjordan.github.io/posts/muon and github.com/KellerJordan/Muon) is built entirely around seeing it.

Muon keeps the same momentum accumulator as classical momentum, then does one more thing before taking the step: it replaces the momentum matrix with the nearest semi-orthogonal matrix — same row/column space, but every singular value pulled toward the same magnitude. In SVD terms, a momentum update $G=U\Sigma V^\top$ becomes $UV^\top$: the directions are kept, the wildly uneven stretch factors $\Sigma$ are thrown away. A raw gradient update for a real weight matrix is typically dominated by a handful of large singular directions; orthogonalizing forces every direction to receive a comparably-sized update, the matrix analogue of what Adam does per-parameter.

Computing an exact SVD every step would be far too slow, so Muon approximates the orthogonalized matrix with a quintic Newton-Schulz iteration — a polynomial recurrence that operates directly on the matrix, no eigendecomposition required:

$$ X_{k+1} = aX_k + b\,X_k(X_k^\top X_k) + c\,X_k(X_k^\top X_k)^2, \qquad (a,b,c) = (3.4445,\,-4.7750,\,2.0315) $$

run for exactly 5 steps starting from $X_0 = G/\|G\|_F$. Those coefficients are deliberately tuned to overshoot the mathematically "safe" convergence radius — the iteration doesn't settle onto a stable fixed point (run it far past 5 steps and singular values drift and oscillate rather than converge), it's tuned purely to pull the singular-value spread tight fast. The widget below shows exactly this: the ratio between singular values collapses to nearly 1 within about 3 iterations, well before the absolute scale has stopped moving around — and 5 iterations is the number Muon actually ships with.

Orthogonalizing an update

Muon's early results were speedrun records, not benchmark papers: a CIFAR-10 speedrun record fell from 3.3 to 2.6 A100-seconds, and it contributed a step in the community's long-running nanoGPT speedrun chain (one record along that chain fell from 31.4 to 24.9 minutes using Muon — part of an 84-record, 45-minute to 1.3-minute lineage, most of which is not attributable to Muon alone). The more controlled number: a 1.5B-parameter model reached GPT-2-XL-level HellaSwag accuracy in 10 H100-hours under Muon versus 13.3 under AdamW — roughly 25% less compute for the same result. Moonshot AI's scale-up study ("Muon is Scalable for LLM Training," arXiv:2502.16982) fit a scaling law across 399M–1.5B parameter models and reported Muon needing only about 52% of AdamW's training FLOPs to match it — call it roughly 2× compute efficiency at that scale, though this hasn't been independently confirmed at trillion-parameter scale.

That trillion-parameter scale is exactly where Muon met a new failure mode: attention logits occasionally exploding mid-training. Moonshot's Kimi K2 (1.04T total / 32B active parameters, a mixture-of-experts model trained on 15.5T tokens, arXiv:2507.20534) fixed it with MuonClip — after each Muon update, compute the largest attention logit produced by each attention head, and if it exceeds a threshold ($\tau=100$), rescale that head's query and key projection weights down by $\gamma_h=\min(1, \tau/S_{\max}^h)$. Applied per-head rather than globally, so healthy heads are left alone. Across the full 15.5T-token run, Kimi K2 reported zero loss spikes; only 12.7% of attention heads ever triggered the clip, and even those stopped needing it after roughly 70,000 steps, once training stabilized on its own.

Aurora: fixing Muon's blind spot on rectangular matrices

Muon's orthogonalization is exact for square matrices, but most transformer weight matrices aren't square — an MLP up-projection might map $d$ dimensions to $4d$. On a genuinely rectangular matrix, equalizing singular values still leaves the resulting update's individual rows at very uneven norms — some neurons keep receiving a healthy update, others receive next to nothing and stop learning almost entirely, a failure Tilde Research calls "neuron death." Their fix, Aurora (Dewulf, Pai, Yang, Zhang & Keigwin, "Aurora: A Leverage-Aware Optimizer for Rectangular Matrices," Tilde Research, May 2026 — blog.tilderesearch.com/blog/aurora, github.com/tilde-research/aurora-release), adds a second constraint on top of Muon's: not just semi-orthogonal, but every row normalized to the same length ($\sqrt{n/m}$ for an $n\times m$ matrix), enforced by alternating between row-normalization and a damped polar-factor step until both hold at once.

This is brand new — released as a blog post and open-source repo, not yet a peer-reviewed paper — so treat the numbers as Tilde's own reported results rather than independently confirmed. On a 1.1B-parameter model trained on roughly 100B tokens, they report a training loss of 2.26 against 2.31 for Muon and 2.33 for NorMuon (a related variant), a HellaSwag score of 67.6% (about 2.5 points above the baselines), and an MMLU score of 37.9% (nearly 11 points above plain Muon at the same scale) — plus a new nanoGPT speedrun record. It's exactly the kind of claim §5 is about: promising, concrete, and not yet independently reproduced.

4. What's special about optimizing at large-model scale

Everything above is true regardless of model size. Four things change qualitatively once the model has tens or hundreds of billions of parameters, and none of them are visible from a small-scale ablation.

Memory stops being an afterthought

Adam's extra state — the momentum and second-moment buffers — is exactly as large as the model itself, twice over. For a 70B-parameter model in bf16, that's roughly 280 GB of optimizer state alone, before the parameters, gradients, or activations. This single fact is why Adafactor exists (T5 was trained with it specifically to avoid this cost), why 8-bit optimizer states are common in practice, and why Lion's one-buffer design turned into a real hardware number: Google reports AdamW needing at least 16 TPU v4 chips to fit ViT-B/16 at batch size 4096, where Lion's smaller state needs only 8.

There's a real ceiling on how much parallelism buys you

Bigger batches mean more GPUs working in parallel per step, which should mean faster wall-clock training — up to a point. OpenAI's gradient noise scale (McCandlish, Kaplan, Amodei et al., arXiv:1812.06162) gives that point a name: the critical batch size, estimated from the ratio of gradient variance to gradient magnitude, $\mathcal{B}_{\text{simple}} = \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)/\|G\|^2$. Below it, doubling the batch size roughly halves the steps needed for free; above it, doubling the batch buys a shrinking fraction of that speedup while doubling the compute bill. The critical batch size itself isn't fixed — it grows by an order of magnitude or more over the course of training, and varies enormously by task: on the order of a few thousand for small image classifiers, on the order of 100,000 tokens for language models, and up into the tens of millions of frames for large-scale RL agents (OpenAI's own Dota 5v5 agent). This is a genuinely large-model-scale concern: small-scale experiments simply never reach the batch sizes where this trade-off bites.

The best learning rate moves when the model gets bigger — unless you fix that

Under the standard way of initializing and scaling a network, the learning rate that works best at one width doesn't transfer to a wider version of the same architecture — which means every new model size has traditionally needed its own expensive hyperparameter sweep. muP (maximal update parametrization; Yang, Hu et al., Microsoft, "Tensor Programs V," arXiv:2203.03466) reparametrizes initialization and per-layer learning rates so that the optimal hyperparameters become provably independent of width. The concrete payoff the paper reports: tuning hyperparameters on a 40M-parameter proxy model under muP and transferring them zero-shot to a 6.7B-parameter target beat the published, separately-tuned GPT-3-scale numbers — using only about 7% of the total pretraining compute that would otherwise have gone into the hyperparameter search itself.

Loss spikes are a real, recurring operational problem

At scale, training loss doesn't always decrease smoothly — it occasionally spikes, sometimes badly enough to derail the run. Google's PaLM report (Chowdhery et al., arXiv:2204.02311) is unusually candid about this: the loss spiked roughly 20 times over the full training run, and each time the fix was operational, not mathematical — restart from a checkpoint roughly 100 steps before the spike, skip the roughly 200–500 data batches that seemed to trigger it, and resume. It's worth correcting a common piece of folklore here: the fix is not to raise Adam's $\epsilon$. Later studies that isolated this specifically (Wortsman et al., arXiv:2309.14322; Molybog et al., arXiv:2304.09871) found the opposite — the default $\epsilon=10^{-8}$ is already too large relative to the tiny gradient magnitudes large, well-scaled models produce, and raising it (to, say, $10^{-6}$) makes instability worse, not better; lowering it (toward $10^{-15}$) is what actually helps.

5. How to evaluate an optimizer

Every year brings a handful of papers claiming a new optimizer beats Adam. Almost none of those claims survive contact with a fair, independent comparison — and the two efforts that actually ran one are worth knowing by name.

"Descending through a Crowded Valley" (Schmidt, Schneider & Hennig, ICML 2021, arXiv:2007.01547) is the closest thing the field has to a controlled trial: 15 optimizers, evaluated across 8 different deep learning problems, over more than 50,000 individual training runs with a standardized tuning budget for every method. Their conclusion, close to verbatim: "Adam remains a strong contender, with newer methods failing to significantly and consistently outperform it." That isn't "nothing has improved since 2015" — it's that most proposed improvements only win on the narrow setting they were introduced to solve, and lose their edge (or lose outright) the moment the comparison broadens.

MLCommons AlgoPerf (Dahl et al., arXiv:2306.07179) turns this into an ongoing, standardized competition rather than a one-time study: fixed workloads, a fixed target loss, and a scored measurement of wall-clock time to reach it, under two rulesets — an external-tuning track (a fixed, generous hyperparameter search budget) and a self-tuning track (no search allowed at all, the algorithm has to just work). In the 2024 competition, the external-tuning winner was a distributed implementation of Shampoo (from Meta), roughly 28% faster than the NadamW baseline; the self-tuning winner was Schedule-Free AdamW, roughly 8–10% faster than baseline with no tuning at all. Both numbers are real, earned improvements — and both are single-digit-to-thirty-percent gains after a genuinely fair fight, a useful anchor against any claim of a 2× or 5× improvement that didn't go through a comparison this rigorous.

The practical checklist this suggests: does the comparison hold the tuning budget fixed across methods (not "well-tuned ours vs. default-hyperparameter theirs")? Does it test more than one model family and more than one dataset? Does it report wall-clock time and not just steps (an optimizer with 3× the per-step cost needs a 3×+ step-count win to actually be faster)? And does anyone other than the method's own authors reproduce the result? Muon and Aurora, above, are honest about exactly where they stand on that last question today.

6. Common design principles

Pull back far enough and every optimizer in this piece is a combination of a small number of recurring moves, applied at a different structural level (per-parameter, per-layer, per-matrix) and traded off against a different cost:

Design moveWhat it buysWho uses it
Average gradients over time Damps noise, accelerates consistent directions Momentum, Nesterov, Adam, Lion, Muon
Adaptive per-parameter scale Normalizes away curvature/gradient-magnitude differences across parameters AdaGrad, RMSprop, Adam, Adafactor
Exact correction for a known bias Fixes the zero-initialization transient without waiting it out Adam's bias correction
Decouple regularization from adaptivity Stops the adaptive term from silently distorting weight decay AdamW, Lion
Operate on the whole matrix, not per-element Sees structure (rank, singular directions) invisible to per-parameter methods Shampoo, SOAP, Muon, Aurora
Cheap iterative approximation of an expensive op Makes an otherwise-intractable matrix operation affordable every step Newton-Schulz (Muon, Aurora), factored second moments (Adafactor)
Trust-ratio / layer-wise rescaling Keeps very large batches stable by matching update size to weight norm per layer LARS, LAMB
Discover the update rule, don't hand-design it Finds counterintuitive but effective forms (e.g. sign-based updates) a human might not try Lion (symbolic program search)

None of these moves are free — every row above trades some combination of memory, compute, or implementation complexity for the property it buys. §1's four axes are the ledger every row gets weighed against.

7. Why this all works

Strip away the specific formulas and there's one underlying idea: a raw gradient is a single noisy sample from a distribution, and almost everything in this piece is a different way of extracting a more reliable signal from a short history of those samples before committing to a step.

Time-averaging trades staleness for lower variance

Momentum's exponential average is a bet that the gradient distribution changes slowly enough that recent history is still informative — true along a consistent slope, false right at a sharp bend, which is exactly why momentum overshoots corners. Adam's second moment makes the same bet about gradient magnitude rather than direction.

Adaptivity is an implicit, cheap curvature estimate

A parameter with consistently large gradients is, roughly, a parameter sitting in a steep direction of the loss; dividing by that history is a rough stand-in for the second-order (Newton's method-style) preconditioning that would require the full, computationally infeasible Hessian. Adam approximates this per-parameter (a diagonal approximation); Shampoo, SOAP, and Muon approximate it per-matrix (capturing cross-parameter correlations a diagonal method structurally cannot see) at higher computational cost.

Every fix here is fixing a specific, nameable failure of the fix before it

SGD → Momentum fixes oscillation in narrow ravines. AdaGrad → RMSprop fixes permanent learning-rate decay. Adam's bias correction fixes a zero-initialization transient. AdamW fixes weight decay's interaction with adaptivity. Muon fixes Adam's blindness to matrix structure. Aurora fixes Muon's blindness to rectangular shape. This is a genealogy, not a pile of unrelated tricks — nearly every method here exists because someone found the exact failure mode of its predecessor and wrote the smallest fix that addressed it.

8. What it means

The honest state of the field in 2026: Adam and AdamW remain the default for good reason — decades of accumulated tuning wisdom, robustness across an enormous range of architectures and scales, and the "Crowded Valley" result that most challengers don't consistently beat it. Muon is the first serious, independently-adopted (Kimi K2, at trillion-parameter scale) challenger in a decade, precisely because it attacks a structural blind spot — matrix shape — that per-parameter methods cannot see by construction, not because it tunes the same knobs harder. Aurora, days old as pieces of technical writing go, is either the next link in that chain or a result that won't replicate outside its own repo — and the honest answer, per §5, is that we don't know yet.

Open problems

  • Does orthogonalization hold up at truly extreme scale? MuonClip's fix for Kimi K2 suggests yes, with a patch — but "the patch we needed at 1T parameters" is exactly the kind of surprise that tends to recur at the next order of magnitude.
  • Memory-vs-precision trade-offs are still being actively renegotiated. 8-bit optimizer states, factored second moments, and single-buffer methods like Lion and Muon are all reactions to the same pressure — optimizer state is now a first-class memory citizen, not a rounding error.
  • Independent replication is the bottleneck, not new ideas. The field produces new optimizer papers constantly; it produces "Crowded Valley"-style fair comparisons rarely, because they're expensive and unglamorous. That asymmetry is exactly why most claimed improvements quietly disappear.

The bottom line

An optimizer is a compressed hypothesis about the shape of the loss landscape and the statistics of its own gradients — plain SGD assumes nothing, Adam assumes gradient scale varies parameter-by-parameter, Muon assumes it varies direction-by-direction within a matrix, Aurora assumes it also varies row-by-row within a rectangular one. Each assumption buys real speed when it holds and real risk when it doesn't, which is exactly why the right response to any new optimizer claim is still the one from §5: what's the fair comparison, and has anyone outside the method's own authors run it?
