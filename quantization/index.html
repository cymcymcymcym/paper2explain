Quantization: how neural networks shrink their numbers

A field guide · number formats & quantization techniques · June 2026

A 70-billion-parameter model stored the honest way — one 32-bit float per weight — needs 280 gigabytes of memory. No single GPU has that. Quantization is the art of writing each of those numbers in fewer bits — 16, 8, 4, sometimes barely more than one — while keeping the model's answers almost unchanged. Do it naively and the model turns to noise. Do it well and the same 70B model runs on a single gaming GPU at 35 GB, eight times smaller, with a quality loss you'd struggle to measure. This is a tour of what those number formats actually are, bit by bit — how to convert between them, and the surprisingly deep bag of tricks that makes 4-bit language models work.

Here is a fact that drives a surprising amount of modern machine-learning engineering: the numbers are the cost. A neural network is, in the end, a giant pile of numbers — the weights — and to run it you must shuttle every one of them out of memory and through an arithmetic unit. How many bits you spend per weight decides how much memory the model occupies, how much bandwidth you burn reading it, and on many chips how fast the math runs. For a large language model, weights so dominate the budget that the format you store them in is one of the highest-leverage choices in the whole system.

The default in training is 32-bit or 16-bit floating point. But once a model is trained, those bits are wildly overspecified. The network has learned a function; it turns out that function barely notices if you describe each weight with 8 bits instead of 16, or even 4 instead of 32. The catch is the word barely. Round the numbers carelessly and the errors compound through a hundred layers until the output is garbage. The entire field of quantization is the study of how to throw away bits on purpose, in exactly the way the network won't mind.

We'll build it from the ground up. First: what a number even is inside the machine — the bit-level anatomy of fp32, fp16, bf16, and the new 8- and 4-bit floats. Then the one tradeoff that governs all of them. Then the mechanics of converting a real number into a low-bit code and back, the villain that makes it hard (outliers), and the menagerie of named techniques — GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant, LLM.int8(), QLoRA — that are all, underneath, answers to the same question. Five widgets and one animation along the way. No prerequisites beyond knowing what a neural-network weight is.

1. How a computer stores a number

A computer cannot store $\pi$. It cannot store $1/3$. It has a fixed number of bits, so it can only store a finite set of values, and every other real number gets rounded to the nearest one it can represent. The question of which finite set is exactly the question of number formats. Floating point is the cleverest answer we have, and it's worth understanding precisely, because every quantization scheme is a variation on it.

A floating-point number is scientific notation in binary. You split the available bits into three fields: one sign bit $s$, a block of exponent bits $e$, and a block of mantissa (or "fraction") bits $m$. The value they encode, for a normal number, is

$$ x \;=\; (-1)^{s}\;\times\;2^{\,e-b}\;\times\;\Big(1+\tfrac{m}{2^{M}}\Big), $$

where $M$ is the number of mantissa bits and $b$ is a fixed bias (so the exponent can be negative). Read it as: the sign flips it, the exponent slides the value up or down by powers of two — that's the "floating" point — and the mantissa, a number between $1$ and just under $2$, fills in the detail within that power-of-two window. The leading $1$ is implicit and free: for a normal number we never need to store it.

The consequence is the single most important fact about floats: the exponent controls how big and small a number you can reach; the mantissa controls how finely you can resolve values once you're there. Range and precision are bought with different bits. Where the formats come from. fp32 (1 sign, 8 exponent, 23 mantissa) is the IEEE-754 single-precision standard from 1985 — the workhorse of scientific computing. fp16 (1·5·10) was standardized for graphics and is precise but narrow: its largest value is only $65{,}504$, so big gradients overflow to infinity. bf16 — "brain float," invented at Google for TPUs — keeps fp32's full 8-bit exponent and amputates the mantissa to 7 bits. It is less precise than fp16 but has the same range as fp32, and that trade turned out to be exactly what deep learning wants, which is why bf16 is now the default training format almost everywhere.

2. The format zoo, and the one tradeoff

Once you internalize "exponent buys range, mantissa buys precision," the whole modern zoo of low-bit formats reads as a single sentence repeated at different budgets. With only 8 bits to spend, the hardware vendors standardized two floats, because there is no single right answer:

  • fp8 E4M3 — 4 exponent, 3 mantissa bits. More precise, narrower range; its largest value is $448$. Used for weights and activations in the forward pass, where precision matters more.
  • fp8 E5M2 — 5 exponent, 2 mantissa bits. Wider range (up to $57{,}344$), coarser. Used for gradients, which span a huge dynamic range and need the headroom more than the resolution.

Go down to 4 bits and the same fork appears: fp4 E2M1 spends its bits on a little range and a little precision, while a plain int4 spends all four on a uniform integer grid. The reason floats keep their charm at low bit-widths is visible the moment you plot the values a format can actually represent. Integers are spaced evenly: the gap between representable values is constant everywhere. Floats are spaced logarithmically: the gap doubles every time the exponent ticks up, so representable values are packed densely near zero and spread out far away.

That non-uniform spacing is a feature. Neural-network weights and activations cluster near zero, with a long thin tail of large values — exactly the shape a float resolves well: fine detail where the mass is, coarse-but-present coverage out in the tail. NF4: a grid shaped like the data. If floats win by matching the data's shape, why not go all the way and build a grid from the data itself? NormalFloat-4 (from the QLoRA work) does exactly that: its 16 levels are the quantiles of a standard normal, so each of the 16 codes is used about equally often when your weights are roughly Gaussian. It's an information-theoretically motivated 4-bit format — no exponent or mantissa fields at all, just a lookup table of 16 carefully chosen numbers.

3. Quantization is snapping to a grid

Strip away the bit-fiddling and quantization is one geometric act: you have a continuous cloud of real numbers, you lay down a discrete grid of representable values, and you snap every real number to its nearest grid point. The leftover displacement — the distance from where a number really was to the grid point it got rounded to — is the quantization error, and essentially all of the field is about making that error small where it matters.

The animation makes the whole story move. A bell-shaped cloud of weights snaps first onto a uniform grid (integer quantization): the rounding errors are the same size everywhere. Then the grid morphs into a non-uniform, float-like grid that is dense near zero and sparse far out: the same weights re-snap, the errors near the center shrink, and the errors in the tail grow. That single picture is the entire range-versus-precision tradeoff.

4. Integers, a scale, and a zero-point

Floats are elegant, but the fastest, cheapest arithmetic on most chips is integer arithmetic — and an 8-bit integer holds only the values $-128 \ldots 127$. To store a weight of $0.0123$ as an integer you obviously can't just round it to $0$. You need a scale: a single floating-point number $s$, shared across a whole tensor, that says "one integer step equals $s$ real units." Optionally you add a zero-point $z$, an integer offset that lets the value $0.0$ land exactly on a grid point even when your data isn't centered. This is affine quantization:

$$ q \;=\; \clip\!\Big(\round\!\big(\tfrac{x}{s}\big) + z,\; q_{\min},\; q_{\max}\Big), \qquad \hat{x} \;=\; s\,(q - z). $$

The first equation quantizes: divide by the scale, round to the nearest integer, shift by the zero-point, and clip to the representable range. The second dequantizes: it reconstructs an approximation $\hat{x}$ of the original. The round trip $x \to q \to \hat{x}$ is lossy, and $\hat{x} - x$ is the quantization error. If you fix $z = 0$ the scheme is symmetric (the grid is centered on zero); a nonzero $z$ makes it asymmetric, which pays for itself when the data is one-sided, like the output of a ReLU.

Everything now hinges on choosing $s$. Too large and you waste your 256 integer codes covering empty range, so nearby weights collapse onto the same code — coarse. Too small and the grid doesn't reach your largest values, so they clip (saturate) to the end of the range — and clipping a big weight to a smaller one is a large, structured error. The textbook starting point is the absmax scale, which sizes the grid to exactly span the biggest magnitude present:

$$ s \;=\; \frac{\max_i |x_i|}{2^{\,b-1}-1}\qquad(\text{symmetric, } b\text{-bit}). $$

Granularity: one scale, or many?

Nothing says the whole tensor must share one scale. The finer you slice, the better each slice fits — at the cost of storing more scales. The standard ladder is: per-tensor (one scale for the entire weight matrix — cheapest, crudest), per-channel / per-row (one scale per output neuron — the workhorse for weights), and per-group / block-wise (one scale per contiguous block of, say, 64 or 128 values — the standard for aggressive 4-bit weight quantization). Finer granularity is the simplest, most reliable lever you have for recovering accuracy, and it's why 4-bit weight quantization in practice almost always means "4-bit values plus a 16-bit scale every 64 weights."

Two axes you'll see named together. What you quantize: weights only ("W4A16" = 4-bit weights, 16-bit activations) versus weights and activations ("W8A8"). Weight-only quantization shrinks the model on disk and in memory and is the easy win; activation quantization is what unlocks fast low-precision matrix multiplies but is much harder, because activations have outliers — the subject of the next section.

5. The villain: outliers

Here is the fact that shapes nearly every serious LLM quantization method. In a large transformer, a handful of activation features — sometimes a fraction of a percent of the channels — carry values tens or hundreds of times larger than everything else. They are not noise; they are systematic and important. But for a per-tensor quantizer they are poison. The absmax scale is set by the single largest value, so a lone outlier of $70$ stretches the grid until the ordinary values near $0.5$ all fall between the same two grid points. You spent your 256 codes describing the outlier and quantized the other 99.9% of the tensor to mush.

There are a few ways out, and the famous method names are mostly different answers to this one problem:

  • Keep the outliers in high precision. LLM.int8() splits each matrix multiply: the few outlier feature-columns are computed in 16-bit, everyone else in int8, and the results are summed. Exact for the hard part, cheap for the rest.
  • Use finer granularity so an outlier only pollutes its own small group rather than the whole tensor — the per-group trick from the last section.
  • Move the difficulty somewhere it's harmless. This is SmoothQuant's idea, and it's elegant enough to deserve its own equation.

Activations are hard to quantize (spiky outliers); weights are easy (smooth, well-behaved). A matrix multiply $Y = XW$ is unchanged if you divide each input channel by some factor and multiply the corresponding weight row by the same factor. So SmoothQuant picks a per-channel smoothing factor $s_j$ that migrates the outlier magnitude out of the activations and into the weights, where the quantizer can cope:

$$ Y \;=\; \big(X\,\diag(s)^{-1}\big)\,\big(\diag(s)\,W\big), \qquad s_j \;=\; \frac{\big(\max_i |X_{ij}|\big)^{\alpha}}{\big(\max_i |W_{ij}|\big)^{1-\alpha}}. $$

The knob $\alpha$ (typically $0.5$) decides how much of the pain to shift. AWQ's variation. Activation-aware Weight Quantization makes the same observation — that a small set of channels is special — but acts only on the weights. It identifies the ≈1% of weight channels that multiply the large-activation features and scales them up before quantizing (and back down after), protecting the weights that actually matter for the output. Same villain, weight-only cure.

6. The menagerie of techniques

With the mechanics and the villain in hand, the named methods fall into a small map. The first split is when you quantize.

Post-training (PTQ) vs. quantization-aware training (QAT)

PTQ takes a finished model and quantizes it, using at most a few hundred unlabeled calibration samples to choose scales — minutes of work, no gradient descent. It's the default for LLMs because retraining a 70B model is out of reach for most people. QAT instead simulates quantization during training: the forward pass rounds the weights ("fake quantization") so the network feels the error and learns weights robust to it. The gradient can't flow through a rounding step — its derivative is zero almost everywhere — so QAT uses the straight-through estimator, which simply pretends the rounding was the identity on the backward pass. QAT recovers more accuracy, especially at very low bit-widths, but costs a training run.

GPTQ: round one weight, fix the rest

The strongest weight-only PTQ methods don't round every weight independently — they round one at a time and compensate. GPTQ quantizes the weights of a layer column by column; after it pins each weight to the grid, it updates all the not-yet-quantized weights in that layer to absorb the error just introduced, using second-order (Hessian) information about how much each weight affects the layer's output. The effect is that errors cancel instead of accumulate, and it's good enough to push large models to 3–4 bits with little damage, in a single pass and no retraining.

NF4 + QLoRA: quantize the frozen base, train a tiny float patch

QLoRA is the method that put fine-tuning of huge models on a single GPU. It freezes the base model in 4-bit NF4, never dequantizing more than one block at a time, and trains only a small pair of low-rank adapter matrices in 16-bit on top. It even quantizes the scales themselves ("double quantization") to claw back the last fraction of a bit. The frozen weights are 4-bit; the gradients only ever touch the tiny high-precision adapter. 

def quantize_tensor(x, bits=8, symmetric=True, group_size=None):
    # choose granularity: one scale, or one per group of `group_size`
    blocks = x if group_size is None else x.reshape(-1, group_size)
    qmax = 2**(bits - 1) - 1                      # e.g. 127 for int8

    if symmetric:
        s = blocks.abs().amax(dim=-1, keepdim=True) / qmax
        z = 0
    else:                                         # asymmetric / affine
        lo, hi = blocks.amin(-1, True), blocks.amax(-1, True)
        s = (hi - lo) / (2**bits - 1)
        z = (-lo / s).round()                     # integer zero-point

    q = (blocks / s).round() + z                  # quantize
    q = q.clamp(-qmax - 1, qmax)                  # clip / saturate
    x_hat = s * (q - z)                           # dequantize (lossy)
    return q.to(int8), s, z, x_hat                # store q, s, z

That's the whole arithmetic core. Every method above is a smarter way of choosing $s$, the granularity, which values to protect, and whether to compensate the survivors — wrapped around these few lines.

7. What you actually save

The payoff is blunt and large. Memory for the weights is just (number of parameters) × (bytes per parameter), so halving the bits halves the footprint. Here is the same 70B model across formats — and what it takes to fit it.

FormatBits / weight70B model sizeQuality
fp32 32 280 GB reference (training default, historically)
fp16 / bf16 16 140 GB lossless in practice — the inference default
int8 / fp8 8 70 GB near-lossless with per-channel + outlier handling
int4 / NF4 ~4.x ≈ 35–40 GB small, often acceptable hit with GPTQ/AWQ + groups

Sizes are weights only, at the nominal bit-width; real 4-bit checkpoints carry a 16-bit scale per group (≈ 4.25–4.5 effective bits), and runtime also needs memory for activations and the KV cache. "Quality" is a qualitative summary of the published consensus, not a single benchmark.

8. Why it works at all

It is genuinely surprising that you can throw away three-quarters of the bits in a trained network and barely move its accuracy. A few reasons conspire.

Networks are trained to be robust to noise

Dropout, data augmentation, stochastic gradients — training is shot through with noise, and the solutions it finds sit in wide, flat basins of the loss landscape where small perturbations to the weights barely change the output. Quantization error is just one more small perturbation. A network that wasn't robust to it would also have failed to generalize.

Errors average out more than they accumulate

Round-to-nearest makes the error on each weight roughly zero-mean and independent. When thousands of them are summed inside a single neuron's dot product, the errors partially cancel rather than reinforce — the sum's relative error shrinks with the square root of the number of terms. The methods that compensate (GPTQ) or shape the grid to the data (NF4, floats) push this further by making the residual error smaller and better-behaved.

Range matters more than precision

This is the deepest lesson, and it's why bf16 beat fp16 for training and why outliers dominate the story. A weight rounded to slightly the wrong value is a small, survivable error. A weight that overflowed its range — clipped to the max, or saturated to infinity — is a catastrophe that no amount of mantissa precision can fix. Almost every design choice in the field, from bf16's wide exponent to SmoothQuant's migration of outliers, is ultimately about protecting range so the precision you do have is spent where the data actually lives.

9. What it means

Quantization is the quiet reason the last few years of AI happened on the hardware we already had. It decoupled how capable a model is from how expensive it is to run: a 4-bit checkpoint on a laptop GPU is the same model that needed a server rack in fp16. That is what put capable language models on phones, in browsers, and inside applications that could never afford a data-center inference bill — and it's why "can it be quantized well" is now a first-class question when a new architecture is designed.

Open problems

  • Below 4 bits. 2- and 3-bit weights, and even 1-bit ("binary"/ternary) networks, work in narrow regimes but generally need quantization-aware training or unusual architectures. Closing the gap to 4-bit quality at 2 bits is an active frontier.
  • Activations and the KV cache. Weight-only quantization is largely solved; quantizing activations and the growing KV cache — both outlier-ridden — to 4 bits without quality loss is much harder and increasingly where the memory actually goes for long contexts.
  • Native low-precision training. The frontier is no longer just compressing a finished model but training in fp8 (and experimentally fp4), where the same range-vs- precision and outlier problems return, now coupled to the dynamics of optimization itself.

The bottom line

Strip away the acronyms and there is one idea here, seen from many angles: you have a tiny budget of bits, the real numbers are spread unevenly, and you must place a finite grid of representable values where it does the most good and snap everything else onto it. Floats shape the grid logarithmically; integers make it uniform and recover range with a scale; granularity slices it finer; GPTQ compensates the survivors; SmoothQuant and AWQ defang the outliers; NF4 molds the grid to the data. Once you see the grid, every method on the menu is just a different, clever way of deciding where to put it.
