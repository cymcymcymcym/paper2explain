A field guide

Data Quality: How Do You Know Your Training Data Is Any Good?

A field guide to judging training data before you train on it · the math of distribution, diversity and distinguishability · deduplication, filtering, and importance resampling · case studies from GPT-3 to FineWeb

Every model is downstream of a pile of text (or images, or trajectories) that someone decided was good enough to learn from — and almost none of that decision is made by reading the data. It's made by a chain of statistical tests: is this document a near-duplicate of one we've already kept? Does it look enough like the kind of text we want? Could a classifier even tell our training set apart from our test set? This is the full toolkit — the math that answers those questions, the methods that apply it at web scale, and what happens when real pipelines get it right (and wrong).

In 2020, a five-line logistic regression classifier — trained on a few hundred thousand Wikipedia and book excerpts as "good" examples versus raw Common Crawl as "bad" ones — decided which few percent of the entire scraped web was worth GPT-3 reading. No architecture search, no extra parameters, no new loss function. Just a filter, applied before training ever started. Half a decade later, the frontier labs still spend more engineering effort on what goes into the model than almost anything that happens once training begins.

That's the uncomfortable truth about "data quality": it isn't a vibe, and it isn't one number. It's a compounding stack of independent statistical tests, each answering a narrower question than it sounds like — is this a duplicate? Does this look like the target domain? Would a simple model even notice if I swapped this document for a random one? — and each with decades of history, from 1970s two-sample statistics to 2023's semantic deduplication papers.

This is the full toolkit: the mathematics for measuring distribution shift, diversity, and distinguishability directly, the concrete methods (deduplication, heuristic and learned filtering, importance resampling) that turn that math into a pipeline, and four real case studies — GPT-3's original web-text classifier, the Hugging Face FineWeb pipeline, LIMA's 1,000-example alignment set, and what happens when you skip deduplication entirely — of what "good data engineering" concretely looks like.

1. Five questions "is this data good?" breaks into

"Data quality" is a folk category that dissolves, on inspection, into several genuinely different questions — and the reason data pipelines have five-plus distinct stages is that no single test answers more than one of them:

  • Validity. Is this a real, parseable document in the language and format you expect, or boilerplate, an error page, a wall of navigation links?
  • Redundancy. Have you (or the model) already seen this, or something close enough to it that a second copy teaches nothing new — and worse, risks being memorized verbatim?
  • Distributional fit. Does this document look like the kind of text you actually want the model to learn from, or is it merely available?
  • Diversity. Across the whole retained set, is there enough genuine variation, or did your filters accidentally converge everything onto one narrow style?
  • Contamination. Does this document leak the answer to a test you're going to grade the model on later?

Every method in this piece is an answer to exactly one of those five questions, and the case studies later on show what happens when a pipeline only answers some of them.

Why not just use more data? Because more low-quality data doesn't average out — a model trained on twice the tokens of mixed quality can lose to one trained on half the tokens of curated quality, because gradient steps on redundant or off-distribution text don't just fail to help, they compete for capacity against the steps that would have helped. See §8.

2. The math: distribution, diversity, distinguishability

Before any specific method, it helps to have precise mathematical language for the three properties every quality method ultimately measures. All three can be computed from nothing but pairwise similarities between data points — which is why the same handful of tools reappears under different names throughout this piece.

Distribution shift: is set B drawn from the same distribution as set A?

The classical tools are information-theoretic divergences — KL divergence $D_{KL}(P\|Q) = \E_{x\sim P}\left[\log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right]$ and its symmetric cousin the Jensen–Shannon divergence — but both require density estimates, which is exactly what you don't have for a pile of raw documents. The tool that avoids this is the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD; Gretton et al., 2012): embed both sets through a kernel $k(x,y)$ and compare only mean embeddings,

$$ \mathrm{MMD}^2(P,Q) = \E_{x,x'\sim P}[k(x,x')] + \E_{y,y'\sim Q}[k(y,y')] - 2\,\E_{x\sim P, y\sim Q}[k(x,y)] $$

which is zero iff $P=Q$ (for a characteristic kernel like the Gaussian RBF) and estimable from finite samples with no density estimation at all — just pairwise kernel evaluations. This is the same quantity that shows up, in disguise, wherever a paper claims a training set "matches" or "doesn't match" a target domain.

Diversity: how many effectively distinct things are in this set?

Counting unique documents is useless — near-duplicates inflate the count without adding information. The Vendi Score (Friedman & Dieng, 2022, arXiv:2210.02410) fixes this by treating diversity as an effective count, borrowing the same "effective number of species" idea from ecology. Build a similarity matrix $K$ over the set (any kernel with $K_{ii}=1$), normalize it to $K/n$, and take the exponential of its von Neumann entropy:

$$ \mathrm{VS}(K) = \exp\!\left(-\sum_i \lambda_i \log \lambda_i \right), \qquad \lambda_i = \text{eigenvalues of } K/n $$

A set of $n$ identical points scores $\mathrm{VS}=1$; $n$ mutually orthogonal (maximally distinct) points scores $\mathrm{VS}=n$. Six clusters of near-duplicates score close to 6, no matter how many total points you have — which is exactly the failure mode raw document counts can't see and deduplication is meant to fix.

Distinguishability: could a classifier even tell these two sets apart?

This is the most operational of the three, because it's literally the question every learned quality filter, DSIR-style importance weight, and contamination check is built on: train (or simulate) a classifier to separate set A from set B, and read off its accuracy. Near 50% (chance, for balanced sets) means the two sets are statistically indistinguishable; near 100% means a simple model can spot the difference every time. The cheapest version that needs no training at all is the classic nearest-neighbor two-sample test (Friedman & Rafsky, 1979; reframed for deep learning as the "classifier two-sample test" by Lopez-Paz & Oquab, 2017): pool both sets, and for every point check whether its single nearest neighbor shares its label. The fraction that do is the test statistic — and it's this same logic, scaled up to a real logistic-regression or fastText classifier over text features, that GPT-3's data pipeline and DSIR's importance weights both run internally (see §5–6).

3. Finding near-duplicates

Exact duplicates are trivial — hash the document, drop repeats. The real problem is near-duplicates: the same news wire copy syndicated to forty sites with different navigation chrome, the same product description template with the noun swapped, the same forum signature block appended to ten thousand unrelated posts. Lee et al., 2022 ("Deduplicating Training Data Makes Language Models Better," arXiv:2107.06499) found that this kind of near-duplication was severe enough in standard LM pretraining corpora that some sequences were emitted verbatim by trained models thousands of times more often than their training frequency would predict — and that deduplicating the training set cut that memorization dramatically while matching or improving validation perplexity, using fewer training steps.

Their method (and the one CCNet, GPT-3, and nearly every large pretraining pipeline since has converged on) is MinHash + Locality-Sensitive Hashing. Break each document into overlapping word- or character-shingles, treat the document as the set of its shingles, and estimate the Jaccard similarity $J(A,B) = |A\cap B| / |A\cup B|$ between any two documents without ever computing the full intersection:

$$ \Pr[\,\min_{s\in A} h(s) = \min_{s\in B} h(s)\,] = J(A,B) $$

for a uniformly random hash function $h$. Run $H$ independent hash functions, keep the minimum hash value each produces for every document (its signature), and the fraction of matching signature slots between two documents is an unbiased estimator of their true Jaccard similarity — one that gets tighter as $H$ grows, by nothing more exotic than the law of large numbers. Bucket documents by bands of their signatures (LSH) and you can find every pair above a similarity threshold across a billion-document corpus without ever comparing most pairs directly. Lee et al.'s own pipeline used signatures of 9,000 hash functions (20 bands of 450) and flagged anything above Jaccard 0.8 — plus a second, complementary pass (EXACTSUBSTR) that used a suffix array to find any verbatim span of 50+ tokens shared across documents, regardless of what surrounded it.

The scale of the problem is easy to underestimate until you measure it: 3.04% of C4's training documents turned out to be near-duplicates of another document in the same corpus, 13.63% of RealNews, and one 61-word sentence in C4 appeared 61,036 times verbatim. The payoff for removing it wasn't just a smaller corpus — a model trained on the deduplicated data emitted memorized 50-token training sequences at 0.189% the rate (NearDup) or 0.138% (ExactSubstr) of a model trained on the original data's 1.926% rate, an order-of-magnitude drop in verbatim memorization, while matching or slightly improving validation perplexity using fewer training steps.

A newer variant skips shingles entirely and works in embedding space. SemDeDup (Abbas et al., 2023, arXiv:2303.09540) embeds every document, k-means clusters the embeddings, and within each cluster removes near-duplicates whose cosine similarity exceeds a threshold — catching paraphrases and reformattings that share no exact shingles at all, which pure MinHash is blind to. On LAION-440M, removing 37% of the data this way cost just 0.08 points of ImageNet accuracy (68.74%→68.66%); removing 50% cost 0.47 points while halving training time — and on several out-of-distribution evals, the smaller, deduplicated set actually scored higher than the full one.

4. Quality filtering: heuristics, perplexity, classifiers

Once duplicates are gone, most of what's left of a raw crawl is still not text you'd want to train on: navigation chrome, keyword-stuffed spam, auto-generated boilerplate, non-language symbol soup from encoding failures. Three families of filter attack this, in roughly increasing order of cost and precision.

Heuristic rules

The cheapest filters are simple, hand-written thresholds — no model required. C4 (Raffel et al., "Exploring the Limits of Transfer Learning...," 2019, arXiv:1910.10683 — the dataset behind T5) keeps only lines ending in terminal punctuation, discards pages under three sentences, drops any page containing a word from a fixed obscenity list, a "lorem ipsum" placeholder, a curly brace (usually code), or the word "Javascript," and deduplicates any three-sentence span that recurs elsewhere in the corpus. That pipeline alone took a 6.1 TB raw crawl down to 745 GB. DeepMind's Gopher / MassiveText pipeline (Rae et al., 2021, arXiv:2112.11446) goes further with a battery of statistical thresholds instead of a wordlist — word count between 50 and 100,000, mean word length between 3 and 10 characters, a symbol-to-word ratio under 0.1, at least 80% of words containing an alphabetic character, a requirement that at least two of a short list of common English stop words appear, and explicit repetition ratios (reject if more than 30% of lines or paragraphs are duplicated within the document). Gopher's authors deliberately skip a wordlist entirely, citing evidence that such lists over-remove text written in minority dialects and about minority identities.

Perplexity filtering

A cheap statistical model can do better than fixed thresholds. CCNet (Wenzek et al., 2019, arXiv:1911.00359) first deduplicates at the paragraph level with a simple hash (removing roughly 70% of raw text on its own), then trains a 5-gram KenLM model per language on Wikipedia and scores every remaining paragraph by perplexity under it — bucketing into "head" (looks like Wikipedia), "middle," and "tail" (doesn't). On one February 2019 crawl, this took 24 TB and 3 billion pages down to 1.5 billion documents across 174 languages, and a BERT model trained only on the resulting English "head" bucket beat one trained on Wikipedia itself by 3.3 points on XNLI (75.9 vs. 72.6). The catch, and the reason no serious pipeline uses perplexity alone: a highly repetitive spam page can score a deceptively low perplexity, because repeated text is trivially predictable. That's why real pipelines pair a perplexity cut with the repetition heuristics above, not either one alone — the same Goodhart's-Law lesson that shows up everywhere a metric becomes a target.

Learned quality classifiers

The most expensive and most precise option: train an actual classifier to score documents by quality. GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020, arXiv:2005.14165) trained a logistic-regression classifier on hashed text features to distinguish curated reference text (WebText, Wikipedia, books) from raw Common Crawl, then kept each crawled document with probability drawn from a Pareto distribution shaped by that classifier's score — a soft cut rather than a hard threshold, tuned so the accepted documents matched WebText's own score distribution. That classifier plus MinHashLSH dedup took 45 TB of crawled text down to 570 GB — roughly 1.3% of the raw input. Years later, Hugging Face's FineWeb-Edu pipeline (Penedo et al., 2024, arXiv:2406.17557) pushed the same idea further: have Llama-3-70B-Instruct label 460,000 pages for educational value on a 0–5 scale, then distill those labels into a small classifier (82% F1 at a keep-threshold of 3) cheap enough to run over the entire multi-trillion-token pool. It kept only 1.3 trillion of FineWeb's 15 trillion tokens — throwing away 92% — and a 1.7B model trained on that sliver still beat one trained on the full set: MMLU rose from 33% to 37%, ARC from 46% to 57%, matching other web datasets' scores using roughly 10× fewer tokens.

Decontamination: does this leak the answer?

This is the fifth question from §1 in its cleanest form — a two-sample distinguishability check between your training set and the specific benchmarks you'll grade the model on. GPT-3's pipeline swept for 13-gram overlaps between training documents and benchmark text, removing a window around every collision — though a bug in that filter left some overlap only partially removed, and by the authors' own count, roughly a quarter of the benchmarks they checked had more than half their examples overlapping the training set (DROP hit 94%). GPT-4's technical report (2023, arXiv:2303.08774) used a coarser but faster substring check — three random 50-character snippets per example — and found contamination ranging from about 1% (GSM8K) to 39% (LSAT), concluding that the measured effect on scores was "generally small and about as often positive as negative." That last finding is easy to misread as "contamination doesn't matter" — it's closer to "we couldn't detect a large effect with this method," which is itself a reason to run a real decontamination pass rather than skip it.

5. Matching a target distribution

Filtering removes bad documents; it doesn't tell you the retained set has the right proportions. If your downstream task looks like formal writing and your raw pool is 90% forum chatter, uniformly filtering out the worst 10% of forum chatter still leaves you with mostly forum chatter. DSIR (Data Selection via Importance Resampling; Xie et al., 2023, arXiv:2302.03169) reframes the problem as exactly what its name says: importance resampling from classical Monte Carlo, applied to text selection.

For every candidate document $x$ in the raw pool, estimate an importance weight

$$ w(x) = \frac{p_{\text{target}}(x)}{p_{\text{raw}}(x)} $$

using two cheap generative classifiers — multinomial models over hashed uni- and bigrams (10,000 buckets), one fit to a sample of the target domain and one fit to the raw pool — then resample the raw pool without replacement, with probability proportional to $w(x)$ (via a Gumbel-top-$k$ trick, so the whole selection is one pass with no rejection sampling). Documents that look like the target relative to how common they are in the raw pool get oversampled; documents that are either off-target or already overrepresented get downweighted. No document is ever manually labeled "good" or "bad" — the target domain (say, Wikipedia plus books) implicitly defines the shape of what "good" means for this specific model. Selecting from The Pile against a Wikipedia+books target, DSIR reached an 82.30% average on GLUE — beating both random selection (80.25%) and a GPT-3-style trained classifier filter (79.85%) — while running on nothing more exotic than a single 96-core CPU node, no GPU required.

The expensive tier: data attribution

Everything so far scores a document in isolation. The most expensive tier asks a sharper question: which specific training examples caused this specific model behavior? Influence functions approximate the counterfactual "how would this model's output change if example $z$ were upweighted?" without retraining, via $I_f(z) = -\nabla f(\theta^*)^\top H^{-1} \nabla L(z,\theta^*)$ — a Hessian-vector product that is intractable at LLM scale unless you approximate it. Anthropic's Grosse et al., 2023 (arXiv:2308.03296) made it tractable with an EK-FAC approximation to the Gauss-Newton Hessian, and found something that only shows up once you scale the search from 810M to 52B parameters: the most influential training examples stop being lexical near-matches and start being thematic ones. Asked why a model resisted being shut down, the 810M model's top influences were simple keyword overlaps; the 52B model's included a passage of HAL's dialogue from 2010: Odyssey Two — no shared vocabulary, just the same underlying scenario. This tier is too expensive to run over a whole pretraining corpus, but it's exactly the tool for the question dedup, filtering, and resampling can't answer: not "is this data good," but "is this the data actually responsible for the behavior you're trying to understand."

6. Case studies: what good data engineering looks like

Individually, dedup and filtering are textbook methods. The interesting engineering is in how real pipelines compose them — and how differently "good data" can look depending on what stage of training you're feeding.

The filtering pipeline funnel

RefinedWeb (Penedo et al., 2023). Toggle each stage on and off and watch what fraction of the raw Common Crawl survives to the final training corpus.

Language ID leaves 48% of the raw crawl; quality heuristics take that down to 23% of the original; deduplication removes another 45% of what's left — ending around 12–13% of the raw crawl. RefinedWeb's whole argument was that this — heuristics and dedup, with no learned classifier at all — was enough on its own: a 1B-parameter model trained on it scored 56.2% on their aggregate benchmark, against 55.7% for C4, 55.0% for OSCAR, and 53.4% for the hand-curated Pile.

7. Why this matters more every year

Two results from the last few years explain why "just add more tokens" stopped being a satisfying answer to a data shortfall.

The first is about repeating what you already have. Muennighoff et al.'s data-constrained scaling laws (2023, arXiv:2305.16264) found that training on up to 4 epochs of repeated data costs almost nothing compared to the same number of unique tokens — repetition has real value, not just redundancy. But that value decays: their fitted "repetition half-life" puts the point of steeply diminishing returns around 15 epochs, and by roughly 40 epochs additional repeats are worth close to nothing. The nuance that matters for this whole piece: in their experiments, filtering to the lowest-perplexity quarter of the data improved loss, while naive deduplication filtering on its own did not — repetition and redundancy are related problems, but they don't have the same fix, which is exactly why this piece has needed five separate tools rather than one.

The second is about what happens when you run out of real data and reach for synthetic data instead. Shumailov et al.'s "model collapse" result (Nature, 2024; arXiv:2305.17493) trains a model, generates text from it, trains the next generation on that generated text, and repeats — a recursive loop that increasingly resembles how a model-saturated web actually works. Each generation's output distribution narrows: rare, correct tail behavior disappears first, then more and more mass concentrates on a shrinking set of "safe" generic outputs. Their OPT-125M/wikitext2 experiment shows perplexity degrading from 20 to 28 across ten generations of this loop — and one qualitative example is hard to forget: a benign prompt about medieval architecture degenerates, by the ninth generation, into a repeating list of jackrabbit color variants. The fix in their experiments wasn't esoteric — keeping just 10% real data mixed into each generation prevented most of the degradation. But it does mean that distinguishing real from model-generated text — precisely the "distinguishability" question from §2 — is quietly becoming as important a data-quality check as deduplication or contamination.

8. What it means

Put the five questions from §1 back together and a pattern falls out: none of them get easier as models get bigger. If anything, scale raises the stakes on every one of them at once — more parameters to memorize a near-duplicate verbatim, more capacity to overfit a contaminated benchmark, more tokens needed to notice a subtly shifted distribution, and (per §7) a shrinking supply of clearly-real data to check any of it against. "Data quality" isn't a preprocessing footnote you can bolt onto a bigger model later — it's a set of statistical tests that has to run before training starts, get cheaper as they get less precise, and compound when chained.

That compounding is also the practical decision rule. Cheap, high-leverage checks come first: exact and near-duplicate removal (§3) is close to free relative to its effect on memorization, and heuristic rules (§4) require no training at all. Perplexity and learned classifiers (§4) cost more but buy real precision — FineWeb-Edu's willingness to throw away 92% of its own tokens only pays off because the classifier is cheap to run at that scale. Decontamination (§4) isn't optional at any budget; it's the one check whose absence you can't detect from your own training curves. Distribution-matching (§5) and data attribution (§5) are the expensive, precise tools — worth it exactly when the marginal document matters more than the aggregate count, which is why LIMA's 1,000 examples and Anthropic's per-example influence search both live at that end of the cost spectrum, not the pretraining end.

The bottom line

"Is this data good?" was never one question with one number. It's five narrower ones — validity, redundancy, distributional fit, diversity, contamination — each with its own mathematics (distribution shift, an effective-count diversity score, a distinguishability test) and its own family of methods (heuristics, perplexity, learned classifiers, MinHash, importance resampling, influence functions). The toolkit doesn't change as models scale up. It just has to run earlier, cheaper, and more automatically than it used to — because the next 10x in model size is now more likely to come from a better filter than a bigger crawl.
