SLAM: mapping a place and finding yourself at the same time

A field guide · visual & graph-based SLAM, with a look at UMI · June 2026

Drop a robot — or a phone, or a drone — into a room it has never seen, with no GPS and no map. To do anything useful it must answer two questions at once: what does this place look like (build a map), and where am I in it (localize). The catch is that each answer needs the other: you can't place yourself without a map, and you can't build a map without knowing where you are. SLAM — Simultaneous Localization and Mapping — is the family of algorithms that cracks this chicken-and-egg problem by solving both together. It's what lets a robot vacuum cover your floor, a headset anchor a hologram to your desk, and — as we'll see — a cheap handheld gripper called UMI turn a GoPro video of someone doing the dishes into training data a robot arm can learn from.

The word simultaneous is doing a lot of work. If someone handed you an accurate map, finding your place in it would be a solved problem — that's just localization, the thing your phone's GPS does outdoors. And if a surveyor told you exactly where the camera was at every instant, stitching its views into a map would be straightforward — that's mapping. SLAM is hard precisely because you get neither for free. You have a stream of noisy sensor readings, and from that alone you must conjure both the map and the path through it, each bootstrapping the other.

Remarkably, this works, and it works well enough to be invisible infrastructure. The same core ideas power autonomous-car localization, drone navigation, augmented-reality headsets, planetary rovers, and the robot learning systems that are reshaping manipulation. We'll build the concepts in order: the state SLAM estimates, why naive dead-reckoning drifts, the two historical paradigms (filtering and smoothing), the modern view of SLAM as a sparse least-squares problem on a factor graph, the magic of loop closure, and the visual front-end that feeds it. Then we'll ground all of it in a real, recent system — UMI — where SLAM is the quiet engine that converts cheap human demonstrations into robot actions. Five interactive widgets and one animation along the way; no robotics background assumed.

1. What SLAM actually estimates

Strip SLAM to its variables and there are just two kinds. The first is the robot's pose over time — not just position but orientation, because a camera pointed north sees a completely different world than one pointed east. In the plane a pose is three numbers $(x, y, \theta)$; in 3D it's six — a position and a 3D rotation — an element of the group $\SE(3)$. A robot's whole trajectory is a sequence of poses $x_0, x_1, x_2, \ldots$

The second kind is the map. In the simplest and most classic form, the map is a set of landmarks — distinctive points in the world at unknown positions $l_1, l_2, \ldots$ (a corner, a textured patch, a tree). Richer maps exist — dense surfaces, occupancy grids, neural fields — but the landmark view exposes the core problem cleanly. SLAM's job is to estimate all of it jointly: every pose and every landmark, from nothing but the sensor stream and the knowledge of how the robot moves and senses.

Why orientation makes it hard. Position errors add up linearly, but a small error in heading rotates everything you do afterward. Mis-estimate your turn by two degrees and ten meters later you're a third of a meter off — and it only grows. That coupling between rotation and position is exactly why drift is so vicious, and why the math lives on rotation groups rather than in flat vector space.

2. Odometry, and why it drifts

The cheapest way to track where you are is dead reckoning: start at a known pose and keep adding up your motion. Wheel encoders count rotations; visual odometry estimates how the camera moved between frames; an IMU integrates acceleration and angular velocity. Each gives you an incremental motion $u_i$, and you chain them: $x_{i} = f(x_{i-1}, u_i)$.

The problem is that every $u_i$ is a little wrong, and the errors accumulate. There's no reference to correct against, so the estimate wanders away from reality — slowly at first, then inexorably. This is drift, and it's the antagonist of the whole field. 3. Two ways to solve it

Historically, SLAM split into two camps over a single question: do you keep only the current estimate, or do you remember and re-optimize the whole history?

Filtering — keep only now

The classic EKF-SLAM maintains one big Gaussian over the current robot pose and every landmark, updating it recursively: a predict step rolls the estimate forward with the motion model, and an update step corrects it with each new measurement. It's online and memory-light in trajectory length — but its covariance matrix grows with the number of landmarks, costing $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ per update, and once a measurement is folded in, its linearization is frozen for good. FastSLAM made this scale with a clever particle filter, but the deeper issue remained: throwing away the past means you can't revisit and fix old mistakes.

Smoothing — remember everything

Modern SLAM keeps the entire trajectory and map as free variables and finds the configuration that best explains all measurements at once. This is graph-based or factor-graph SLAM, and it won — because the problem turns out to be enormously sparse (each pose touches only its neighbors and the few landmarks it saw), and sparse nonlinear least squares can be solved astonishingly fast. It's also what makes loop closure natural: a new constraint between old poses simply gets added to the graph and the whole thing re-optimizes.

4. SLAM as one big least-squares problem

Here is the modern formulation in full. Collect all unknowns — poses and landmarks — into one big vector $X$. We want the most probable $X$ given all measurements $Z$, the maximum-a-posteriori estimate:

$$ X^\star \;=\; \argmax_X\ p(X \mid Z) \;\propto\; p(x_0)\,\prod_i p\big(x_i \mid x_{i-1}, u_i\big)\,\prod_k p\big(z_k \mid x_{i_k}, l_{j_k}\big). $$

That product has one factor for the prior, one motion factor per odometry step (how consistent are consecutive poses with the measured motion $u_i$?), and one observation factor per measurement (how consistent is the predicted view of landmark $l_{j_k}$ from pose $x_{i_k}$ with what was actually seen, $z_k$?). Drawn out, variables and factors form a factor graph.

Assume the noise on each factor is Gaussian. Taking the negative log turns the product into a sum and each Gaussian into a squared error, so MAP estimation becomes nonlinear least squares:

$$ X^\star \;=\; \argmin_X\ \sum_i \big\lVert f(x_{i-1}, u_i) \ominus x_i \big\rVert^2_{\Lambda_i} \;+\; \sum_k \big\lVert h(x_{i_k}, l_{j_k}) - z_k \big\rVert^2_{\Sigma_k}, $$

where each term is a Mahalanobis norm $\lVert e\rVert^2_\Sigma = e^\top \Sigma^{-1} e$ that weights an error by how certain that measurement is. You minimize it by repeated linearization — Gauss-Newton or Levenberg-Marquardt — solving a linear system at each step:

$$ \big(J^\top \Sigma^{-1} J\big)\,\Delta X \;=\; -\,J^\top \Sigma^{-1}\, e. $$

The matrix on the left is the information matrix, and because the factor graph is sparse — most pairs of variables share no factor — it is sparse too. Exploiting that sparsity (with sparse Cholesky factorization, and incrementally in systems like iSAM/GTSAM) is what makes optimizing thousands of poses and landmarks feasible in real time. This least-squares-on-a-graph view is the backbone under essentially every modern SLAM and structure-from-motion system.

5. Loop closure: the move that works

Everything so far still drifts — the factor graph of pure odometry is just a chain, and a chain of noisy links sags away from the truth. Loop closure is what changes everything. When the robot recognizes that it is seeing a place it has visited before, it can add a brand-new factor directly connecting two poses that are far apart in time but close in space. That single constraint says "these two moments are at the same place," and when the graph re-optimizes, the accumulated drift gets distributed backward around the entire loop. The map snaps into global consistency.

It is genuinely dramatic to watch. The animation shows a drifted loop — the trajectory wandering, each landmark smeared into two sightings — the instant a loop closure is recognized, and the optimization pulling the whole structure into a single coherent map.

A drifted pose graph: nodes are poses, edges are odometry constraints. Add the loop-closure constraint and step the Gauss-Newton optimizer. Watch the drifted loop pull shut and the total squared error drop — that's SLAM correcting itself.

How does it know it's been here before? That's place recognition, a front-end job: summarize each image as a bag of visual words and look for a past image that matches. A false loop closure is catastrophic — it welds together two places that aren't the same — so systems verify a geometric consistency check before trusting one.

6. The front-end: turning pixels into constraints

The least-squares back-end assumes someone has already produced the measurements and told it which measurement corresponds to which landmark. Producing those is the front-end, and for cameras it's most of the practical work: detect distinctive features (corners, blobs — ORB, SIFT), match them across frames (data association), and reject outliers. Get the association wrong and the back-end optimizes a fiction.

Triangulation and why baseline matters

A single camera sees only a direction to a landmark — a ray, not a distance. See the same landmark from two poses and the two rays intersect, fixing its 3D position by triangulation. The wider the baseline between the views (and the more views), the sharper that intersection: the landmark's uncertainty ellipse shrinks. Move the camera in the widget and watch a landmark's estimate tighten as the rays cross at a healthier angle.

Triangulate a landmark

Each camera pose contributes a noisy bearing ray to the landmark. Drag the camera baseline and add views; the rays intersect to estimate the landmark, and its uncertainty ellipse shrinks as the geometry improves. A narrow baseline gives a long, uncertain ellipse.

The scale problem

A single moving camera has a famous blind spot: it cannot recover absolute scale. A small object seen up close and a large object seen far away project to the very same pixels. Formally, scaling the whole world and the camera's translation by any factor $s$ leaves every image identical:

$$ \pi\big(K(R\,p + t)\big) \;=\; \pi\big(K(R\,(s\,p) + s\,t)\big)\qquad\text{for any } s > 0, $$

where $\pi$ is the perspective divide and $K$ the camera intrinsics. So monocular SLAM reconstructs the world only up to an unknown scale. To pin it down you need a metric reference: a stereo pair with a known baseline, a depth (RGB-D) sensor, an object of known size — or an IMU, whose accelerometer feels true gravity and acceleration in metric units. Toggle the scale anchor in the widget and watch the ambiguous world lock to a real size.

Monocular scale ambiguity

The same camera images are consistent with a small-and-near world or a large-and-far one — slide the scale and the picture in the camera doesn't change. Switch on a metric anchor (stereo / depth / IMU) and the true scale gets fixed.

7. UMI: SLAM as a data factory

Here's where it gets concrete and a little surprising. One of the biggest bottlenecks in robot learning is data: teaching a robot to manipulate objects usually means painstaking teleoperation on real hardware. The Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) sidesteps this with a beautifully simple device — a cheap handheld gripper with a GoPro bolted on — that anyone can pick up and use to demonstrate a task in the wild, no robot required. Wash a cup, open a drawer, arrange flowers; the GoPro just records.

But a video isn't an action. To train a policy, you need the gripper's full 6-DoF trajectory — where it was and how it was oriented at every instant — plus how open its fingers were. UMI recovers all of this from the GoPro footage alone, and the engine that does it is visual-inertial SLAM (UMI runs ORB-SLAM3 on the fisheye video and the GoPro's IMU). SLAM turns "a phone-quality video of a hand" into a metric, time-stamped path the robot can replay.

The last link is a rigid hand-eye transform: the GoPro is bolted to the gripper, so the constant transform $T^{\text{cam}}_{\text{gripper}}$ from camera frame to gripper-tip frame is known by calibration. Compose it with the SLAM pose and you get the end-effector pose in the world:

$$ T^{\text{world}}_{\text{gripper}} \;=\; T^{\text{world}}_{\text{cam}} \cdot T^{\text{cam}}_{\text{gripper}}. $$

Two SLAM-specific details make UMI work in practice, and both are themes from earlier. First, scale: a lone GoPro is monocular and scale-blind, so UMI leans on the IMU (and a known scene/​gripper geometry) to recover metric trajectories — a robot needs centimeters, not arbitrary units. Second, a wide fisheye lens gives the tracker lots of stable features and robustness to the fast, jerky motions of a human hand. 8. Why it works

Three ideas carry the whole edifice, and they're worth holding onto.

Sparsity makes the impossible cheap

Jointly optimizing thousands of poses and landmarks sounds hopeless, but almost every pair of variables is unrelated — a pose constrains only its time-neighbors and the handful of landmarks it saw. The information matrix is therefore mostly zeros, and sparse linear algebra solves it in roughly linear time. SLAM scales because the world is local.

Loop closure beats drift

Odometry's error grows without bound; a single global constraint resets it. The reason SLAM produces consistent maps over kilometers, while raw odometry smears, is that revisiting places injects information that no amount of careful dead-reckoning can. Mapping isn't just integration — it's integration plus correction.

Fuse complementary senses

No single sensor is enough: cameras are rich but scale-blind and slow; IMUs are fast and metric but drift quickly; LiDAR is precise but sparse in texture. SLAM's least-squares core is, at heart, a principled way to fuse them — each becomes a factor with its own uncertainty, and the optimizer lets the confident measurements dominate where they should. UMI's camera-plus-IMU is exactly this bargain.

9. What it means

SLAM is one of those rare ideas that graduated from research problem to invisible infrastructure. It's in your vacuum, your headset, every self-driving stack, and — the part most people miss — increasingly in the data pipeline of modern robot learning. UMI is the tell: by making 6-DoF tracking cheap and portable, SLAM turns the entire physical world into a place where manipulation data can be collected, by anyone, with a gripper and a GoPro. The algorithm that lets a robot know where it is has quietly become the algorithm that lets robots learn what to do.

Open problems

  • Dynamic and changing scenes. Classic SLAM assumes a static world; people, cars, and moving objects break that assumption, and robustly mapping places that change over time is far from solved.
  • Dense and semantic maps. Landmarks localize well but don't tell you that a surface is a table or a door. Fusing SLAM with dense reconstruction (including neural and Gaussian-splat maps) and semantics is an active frontier.
  • Lifelong, multi-session mapping. Merging maps across days, devices, and lighting, and keeping them consistent and compact forever, remains genuinely hard — and is exactly what large-scale data collection à la UMI will demand.

The bottom line

Underneath the acronyms, SLAM is a single, elegant bargain: write every noisy thing you know — each motion step, each sighting, each "I've been here before" — as a weighted constraint in one big sparse graph, then find the trajectory and map that bend to satisfy all of them at once. Odometry guesses; loop closure corrects; least squares reconciles. The result is a machine that, dropped into an unknown place, can answer both where am I and what is this place — and, increasingly, hand that answer to a robot as something to learn from.
